## Concept 1 — The Ball That Climbs the Ramp

**Character:** Mimi (active canonical registry ID `00000000-0000-0000-0000-000000000003`). The registry confirms the character identity. Mimi is described by the owner as a light-blue rabbit; a verified visual reference binding is still **UNKNOWN** for this concept branch.
**Primary object:** One large, solid-color play ball.
**Single impossible rule:** On one broad ramp, the ball always rolls uphill under its own motion, without a visible push.
**Viewer promise:** A character tries to stop one ball from reaching the top of a ramp, but the ball keeps choosing the climb.

### Frame zero

A single broad ramp crosses the vertical frame from low foreground to high background. Mimi is beside its lower third, facing the viewer enough to read the reaction. The ball is already rolling uphill in the first frame: its seam has slight rotational blur, it is separated from Mimi's hands, and the ramp surface is plainly inclined. No hand, string, motor, second ball, text, or preceding setup is visible. The uphill direction is clear from the ramp geometry and ball motion; the audience need not infer a preceding push. This is a visual premise, not proof of how a model will render it.

### Fifteen-second structure

| Time | Character action | Physical consequence and escalation |
|---|---|---|
| 0–2s | Mimi spots the ball already climbing and leans toward it. | The first frame establishes the impossible motion. Keep the camera wide enough to show both the full ramp direction and the ball. |
| 2–5s | Mimi places one open paw gently in the ball's path at the lower third. | The ball presses against the paw and pauses. The contact is a single, readable stop; Mimi does not push it uphill. |
| 5–8s | Mimi slowly lifts the paw clear and watches. | With no visible impulse, the ball resumes rolling uphill. This confirms the rule after the opening hook rather than adding a second rule. |
| 8–11s | Mimi hurries beside the ramp and reaches the upper end before the ball. | The ball continues at a steady, moderate pace. Mimi arrives in time without running into or stepping across its path. The pursuit changes the character's position, not the ball's rule. |
| 11–15s | Mimi offers an open paw at the upper lip and catches the ball softly. Mimi remains bent down at the lower end of the ramp, arm stretched up its length. | The ball rests in the raised paw. End on the long diagonal: Mimi low, one ball high, empty ramp between them. The earned payoff is the completed climb and the deliberately awkward posture, not a last-second reversal. |

### Production assessment

- **Thin objects:** None in the core gag. Keep the ball a large, matte, high-contrast sphere without small decals.
- **Contact continuity:** One brief paw-to-ball stop and one catch. The ball should not penetrate the paw, split, or change size.
- **Object identity:** Only one ball; preserve color, size, and seam orientation cues across the continuous path. No multiplication or extra balls.
- **Occlusion:** Keep Mimi beside the ramp. Do not let Mimi's body hide the ball during the uphill movement.
- **Complex motion:** A single rolling trajectory on a static, broad ramp. Avoid spins, jumps, rebounds, or camera moves.
- **Count tracking:** One ball throughout; count risk is low.
- **Temporal overload:** Five clear beats, one attempt to stop, one resumed climb, and one catch. The chase is a small positional change, not another trick.
- **480p first-frame readability:** Strong if the ball is at least about one tenth of frame height, ramp edges are broad, and the upward slope spans most of the frame. Exact pixel performance is **UNKNOWN** until a still is rendered and inspected.
- **Likely Seedance failure modes:** The ball may roll downhill according to ordinary gravity; it may appear pushed by Mimi or an unseen force; the ramp angle may drift; the ball may slide rather than rotate; the hand catch may warp. A single continuous take reduces cut-to-cut continuity burden but does not eliminate these risks.
- **Character reference:** A verified visual reference is **UNKNOWN**; bind one before character-identity evaluation or any render authorization.

### Differentiation and nearest prior engine

This is an **inverted-gravity pursuit** with a one-object path and a catch at the high point. The opening anomaly is motion against a visible incline; the escalation is the character repositioning to intercept; the final silhouette is a long low-to-high diagonal. It does not depend on multiplication, a door, an opening/closing container, a missing item, or a chair inversion.

An adult viewer may pause to resolve whether the ball is being secretly pushed. A second watch could inspect the hand contact and the ball's rotation during the release; this is a designed curiosity, not a retention claim. The closest historical Pompom Hills engine is the ball-and-crocodile multiplication short because both use a ball as the central visual object. **Duplicate-silhouette risk: MEDIUM** for the shared ball motif; **structural-engine risk: LOW** because this has one ball, no mouth, no quantity growth, no bursts, and no surrounding-ball pile. ## Concept 2 — Kiko's One-Beat-Late Shadow

**Character:** Kiko (active canonical registry ID `00000000-0000-0000-0000-000000000002`). The registry confirms Kiko is an active canonical name; its visual design and verified reference binding are **UNKNOWN** for this concept branch.
**Primary object:** Kiko's single, dark, attached floor shadow.
**Single impossible rule:** The shadow copies Kiko's pose and movement exactly one beat later. It remains a shadow attached to Kiko, not a second character.
**Viewer promise:** Kiko tests a shadow that seems to remember each gesture a moment too late.

### Frame zero

Use a simple, brightly lit floor and a plain wall behind Kiko, with one broad, readable light direction. Kiko stands centered with one limb raised overhead. The attached shadow is large and high-contrast, but the corresponding limb is still down at the side. The mismatch is present in the first image; it does not require a prior wave or camera cut to understand that the shadow is wrong. Avoid visible lamps, windows, furniture silhouettes, or other shadow-casting props.

### Fifteen-second structure

| Time | Character action | Physical consequence and escalation |
|---|---|---|
| 0–2s | Kiko holds the raised-limb pose, then glances down. | The shadow remains in its earlier pose. The static mismatch supplies the opening anomaly. |
| 2–5s | Kiko lowers the limb and raises it once, slowly. | After a clear pause of roughly one beat, the shadow copies the change. The lag is demonstrated with one large limb silhouette. |
| 5–8s | Kiko makes one quick side-step and turns the torso toward the wall. | The shadow first completes the previous pose, then follows the step and turn with the same delay. It stays attached at Kiko's ground contact point and keeps one consistent light direction. |
| 8–11s | Kiko freezes abruptly, arms at sides. | The shadow completes the last delayed turn before settling into Kiko's current still pose. No extra shadow, independent gesture, or new physical rule appears. |
| 11–15s | Kiko carefully lifts one limb again, then lowers it and waits. | The shadow performs that final lift and lowering one beat later. End with Kiko looking down at the now-matching silhouette, one limb half-raised as if deciding whether to try again. The payoff is delayed confirmation, not a fake escape. |

### Production assessment

- **Thin objects:** No thin prop. Keep the raised limb clearly separated from the body silhouette; map the exact pose to Kiko’s verified design.
- **Contact continuity:** No hand-object contact. The shadow must remain attached to the feet and on the same floor plane.
- **Object identity:** One shadow only; do not turn it into a duplicate character, reflection, separate creature, or cast shadow from an unseen second light.
- **Occlusion:** Keep Kiko's body and shadow side by side with enough floor visible. Avoid the shadow passing behind furniture or other silhouettes.
- **Complex motion:** Three deliberately simple poses and one side-step. Use clear holds to communicate the delay. Do not ask for independent shadow choreography beyond replaying Kiko's own movements.
- **Count tracking:** No countable objects; low risk.
- **Temporal overload:** The rule is temporal, so each beat needs a visible hold. If the delay is too short at 15 seconds, the gag collapses; if too long, the sequence may feel inert.
- **480p first-frame readability:** Good if the pose mismatch occupies a large portion of frame and the shadow is dark against a clean floor. Contrast must survive small-screen viewing. Exact readability is **UNKNOWN** until frame inspection.
- **Likely Seedance failure modes:** The shadow may immediately track Kiko normally; its shape may change without the matching body pose; the delay may vary; it may detach from the feet; lighting direction may flip; the generator may add a second shadow. A continuous fixed camera and large, slow poses reduce but do not remove these risks.
- **Character reference:** A verified visual reference is **UNKNOWN**; visual identity remains a separate gate.

### Differentiation and nearest prior engine

This is a **temporal echo in a single attached silhouette**, unlike the ramp's spatial motion against gravity or the bowl's locked material orientation. Opening: two simultaneous poses disagree. Escalation: the viewer sees one then several delayed correspondences, culminating in a held final echo. Ending: body and shadow re-align after the final gesture. The adult-first curiosity is whether the mismatch will resolve or become independent; the planned reveal answers only the delay mechanism. Replay could reveal whether each shadow pose follows exactly one beat later, not a claim of audience performance.

The closest historical Pompom Hills engine is the familiar mirror/reflection reaction used in the sticky-note source. **Duplicate-silhouette risk: MEDIUM** because a shadow/reflection mismatch can resemble a mirror gag. The distinction must remain explicit: there is no mirror, no reflected room, and the shadow copies the character's own movement after a fixed delay. If a visual reference shows a similar delayed reflection/shadow premise, reject this concept as too close.

## Concept 3 — The Bowl That Keeps Its Own Horizon

**Character:** Luca (active canonical registry ID `00000000-0000-0000-0000-000000000005`). The registry confirms Luca is an active canonical name; its visual design and verified reference binding are **UNKNOWN** for this concept branch.
**Primary object:** One wide, shallow, opaque-sided bowl containing a single visible pool of vivid blue water.
**Single impossible rule:** The water surface is rigidly locked to the bowl's orientation; when the bowl tilts, the water tilts with it and stays inside.
**Viewer promise:** Luca turns a bowl almost sideways, but its water keeps the bowl's angle instead of finding level.

### Frame zero

Luca holds a broad, shallow bowl at a clear 35–45 degree angle, with the open rim facing the camera. A saturated blue water plane is visibly tilted at the same angle as the bowl, and the liquid's edge remains inside the lower rim rather than settling level or spilling. Frame the bowl large in the foreground and Luca immediately behind it; exact facial design is UNKNOWN until a verified reference is bound. The bright blue surface against a warm neutral bowl makes the wrong orientation legible without text or earlier action. A still frame can show the anomaly directly.

### Fifteen-second structure

| Time | Character action | Physical consequence and escalation |
|---|---|---|
| 0–2s | Luca holds the tilted bowl steady and looks from the water to the horizon. | The bowl-relative water angle is visible immediately. There is no pour or splash to obscure the initial state. |
| 2–5s | Luca slowly tilts the bowl a little farther to one side. | The water plane rotates with the bowl and remains inside the rim, rather than returning to level. One smooth movement tests the rule. |
| 5–8s | Luca rotates the bowl toward a near-vertical orientation, keeping its opening visible to camera. | The water stays as one coherent sheet inside the bowl. Do not create droplets, streams, or a second water surface. |
| 8–11s | Luca turns the bowl through a sideways orientation and pauses. | The water remains fixed relative to the ceramic form. The increasing angle makes the rule more obvious without adding a second action. |
| 11–15s | Luca slowly returns the bowl to a stable diagonal and holds it away from the body. | The surface remains slanted with the bowl. Luca gives a cautious, puzzled reaction (exact expression design UNKNOWN); end on the diagonal bowl and matched water plane. No spill, cleanup, or false resolution is added. |

### Production assessment

- **Thin objects:** No thin prop. The water boundary is a broad, simple shape, not a fine stream or droplets.
- **Contact continuity:** Luca maintains one consistent visible grip throughout; the exact grip and appendages are **UNKNOWN** until mapped to a verified Luca reference. Keep contact outside the rim and avoid grip changes mid-turn.
- **Object identity:** One bowl and one connected pool of water. Preserve the bowl rim and fill shape. No refilling, duplication, color change, or hidden vessel.
- **Occlusion:** Keep the open bowl angled toward camera and clear of Luca's face. Keep the water line unobstructed by the character or bowl rim.
- **Complex motion:** Slow rotation in place, under a half-turn, with pauses at two angles. Avoid a full spin or inversion; full inversion increases liquid-containment and opening-orientation ambiguity without adding much story value.
- **Count tracking:** One pool; no droplets. Low count risk if splashes are prohibited.
- **Temporal overload:** One rule demonstrated through graded orientation, not repeated attempts. The risk is visual sameness across the angle changes; vary Luca's reaction and hold lengths, not the core action.
- **480p first-frame readability:** Medium. The blue surface must occupy enough area and have a sharp boundary against the bowl. At low resolution, a steeply tilted water shape can be misread as a colored insert. Exact legibility is **UNKNOWN** until a still is rendered and inspected.
- **Likely Seedance failure modes:** Water may obey normal gravity and spill; the water plane may remain horizontal while the bowl rotates; the surface can turn into solid blue material or slosh into multiple shapes; the bowl may warp or change size; hand grips may drift. This concept has the highest material-simulation burden of the three.
- **Character reference:** A verified visual reference is **UNKNOWN** and is needed separately.

### Differentiation and nearest prior engine

This is a **material-orientation lock**, not multiplication, temporal delay, or uphill motion. Its opening is a static tilted surface; escalation is progressive bowl rotation; ending preserves the same impossible relation at a clear diagonal. Adult curiosity is whether the liquid remains attached as the vessel rotates. An intentional second watch could inspect whether the water surface truly rotates with the bowl or merely wobbles; no audience behavior is asserted.

The closest historical Pompom Hills engines are the snack-box contents changing on repeated openings and the sticky-note multiplication story: both involve a container-like object and staged tests. **Duplicate-silhouette risk: LOW to MEDIUM** for “character tests an object repeatedly”; **engine risk: LOW** because the bowl does not open, change contents, multiply, or produce a new item. Keep one continuous tilt sequence and no repeated opening/closing rhythm to preserve this distinction.

**Recommended strongest proposal for later visual exploration: `The Ball That Climbs the Ramp`.** It offers the clearest action-to-consequence chain with one opaque, high-contrast object, no small-object choreography, no counting progression, and a simple catch payoff. Its first-frame anomaly can be demonstrated in motion without explanatory text. The recommendation is for a disposable visual feasibility study only; it is not an approval to generate an image, video, or production render. Its ball motif is a genuine duplicate-risk check, and the feasibility of uphill motion and the catch remains unverified.

The bowl concept is structurally distinct but asks the video model to preserve a nonphysical liquid boundary through several rotations. Its current beat sequence risks reading as repeated angle changes rather than a rising story, so it is the least production-safe and weakest progression draft at 480p.
